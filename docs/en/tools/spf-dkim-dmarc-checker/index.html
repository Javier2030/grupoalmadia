The invoice scam

Spoofing your email does not require your password.

Email was designed without verifying who the sender is. Any server can claim "this message comes from management@yourcompany.com". The only thing that stops it is three records that you publish on your domain. If they are missing or incomplete, the fake message can get through.

01

The scammer chooses a target

Your customers, your accountant, your bank or your own payments team. Their names are often on your website and social media.

02

They send the email from your exact address

Not a look-alike domain: yours. An invoice with a different bank account, a "change of payment details" or an urgent order from the manager.

03

The recipient's server checks your domain

It asks whether that sender is authorized and what to do if it is not. If your domain returns nothing, the message may be delivered.

04

The customer pays and holds you responsible

The money went to another account, and the email appeared, to everyone's eyes, to come from your company. That is where the commercial and legal trouble begins.

What the tool checks

Three records decide whether your email can be forged.

All three are published in your domain's DNS and are public: any mail server reads them before accepting a message. The tool performs that same lookup and translates it for you.

The score from 0 to 100 is weighted as follows: DMARC 45, SPF 25, DKIM 15 and additional layers 15. The risk verdict depends mostly on DMARC, because it is the only one of the three that instructs servers to block.

How to read the result

What each risk level means.

High risk

  • Your domain has no DMARC, or has it in monitoring mode ("p=none").
  • Today, a fake email using your address can reach the inbox.
  • This is the most common situation among small businesses, and it can be fixed without changing your email provider.

Medium or low risk

  • Medium: a policy that takes action is already in place (quarantine), but gaps remain: it applies to only part of your mail or is in test mode, subdomains are not covered, or SPF authorizes anyone.
  • Low: your domain instructs servers to reject fake emails. What remains is to confirm that all your sending services sign their messages and to read the reports.
  • In both cases, the details tell you what to improve, from most to least important.
Why it matters now

It is no longer optional: the major mailbox providers require it.

  • Google (since February 2024): requires SPF, DKIM and DMARC from anyone sending more than 5,000 emails a day to Gmail accounts, and at least SPF or DKIM from everyone else. Yahoo applies equivalent rules to bulk senders.
  • Microsoft (since May 2025): requires SPF, DKIM and DMARC from domains sending more than 5,000 emails a day to Outlook, Hotmail and Live.
  • Deliverability: a domain without these records is not only easier to spoof; its legitimate emails, including invoices and quotes, are more likely to land in the spam folder.
  • Data protection: no law mentions DMARC by name, but regulations such as the GDPR in Europe (Article 32) or Law 1581 of 2012 in Colombia require anyone who processes personal data to take appropriate technical measures against unauthorized or fraudulent use. A spoofable company email address is an obvious weak point.
  • Pursuing the scammer is possible, but slow. In Colombia, for example, Law 1273 of 2009 punishes website spoofing to capture data and the unauthorized transfer of assets. Closing the door on them takes a few weeks.
Being upfront

What this check does not tell you.

  • It does not check mailboxes or passwords. If someone on your team has had their password stolen, the email really does come from their account and no DNS record can stop it. That is closed off with two-factor authentication.
  • It does not detect look-alike domains. A scammer can register yourcompany-payments.com. DMARC protects your domain, not the ones that resemble it.
  • DKIM results are not conclusive. Keys cannot be listed from the outside; if yours has an uncommon name, it will not appear.
  • It is not a penetration test or a certified audit. It is a reading of your domain's public configuration, which is where most email fraud begins.
  • A good score does not guarantee delivery. Providers also look at the sender's reputation and the content.
Frequently asked questions

What people ask us about email spoofing.

What does it mean when someone spoofs my email?

It means someone sends a message with your exact address in the "From:" field, for example billing@yourcompany.com, without ever having accessed your mailbox. They do not need your password: it is enough that your domain does not tell mail servers to reject messages that do not come from you. With that fake email, they request payments, change the account number on an invoice or steal passwords.

How do I know if my domain is protected?

Enter it in the checker on this page. In a few seconds it tells you whether your domain publishes SPF, DKIM and DMARC, and above all whether your DMARC policy actually blocks fake emails or only monitors them. If the result is "high risk", an email can be sent in your name today.

What is the difference between SPF, DKIM and DMARC?

SPF is the list of servers authorized to send email using your domain. DKIM is a digital signature that proves the message came from one of your services and that nobody altered it. DMARC ties the two together and gives the instruction: what to do with an email that uses your domain and fails both checks for that domain (let it through, send it to spam or reject it). Without DMARC, the other two do not prevent spoofing.

I have DMARC set to "p=none". Am I protected?

No. "p=none" is monitoring mode: your domain requests no action against fake messages, which can still get through. If the record requests reports, you receive details of who is sending email in your name. It is the right first step, and it should be raised in stages to quarantine and then to reject once the reports confirm that your legitimate mail passes.

Why not set "p=reject" right away?

Because if any of your legitimate services (electronic invoicing, newsletters, CRM, website forms) sends email in your name without being properly configured, your own messages would be rejected. That is why it is done in stages, reading the reports before each change.

Does the tool store my domain or my data?

No. The check runs in your browser: queries go directly to the public DNS resolvers of Google and Cloudflare, which receive the domain name as they would in any DNS query. Almadía does not receive the domain you enter, does not ask for an email address or sign-up, and the page has no analytics. We only find out if you decide to message us on WhatsApp.

Is this a penetration test?

No. The tool only reads DNS records, which are public by design: it is the same information any mail server looks up before accepting a message. It does not touch your systems, does not try passwords and does not send any email. That is why you can also check the domain of a supplier or a customer.

My email is @gmail.com or @hotmail.com. Is this useful for me?

This check is for your own domains (yourname.com, yourcompany.com). If you use a free account, spoofing protection is managed by the provider and you cannot change it. For a business, having email on its own, properly configured domain is the first step.

Why does it say it found no DKIM when I do have it?

DKIM keys cannot be listed from the outside: they are published under a name ("selector") chosen by each provider. The tool tries the most common names; if yours is different, it will not appear. If you know it, enter it in the optional selector field. You can also confirm it by looking at the header of a real email sent from your domain.

Do Google and Microsoft require this?

Yes, from high-volume senders. Since February 2024, Google has required SPF, DKIM and DMARC from anyone sending more than 5,000 emails a day to Gmail, and Yahoo applies equivalent rules to bulk senders. Since May 2025, Microsoft has required the same from anyone sending more than 5,000 a day to Outlook, Hotmail and Live. For smaller senders, Google requires at least SPF or DKIM. In practice, a domain without these records increasingly ends up in the spam folder.
